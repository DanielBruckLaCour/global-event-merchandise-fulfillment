# Global Event Merchandise Fulfillment

**An operational framework for planning, producing, coordinating, and delivering branded merchandise across multiple countries.**

## Overview

Managing branded merchandise for an international event requires coordination across products, suppliers, production locations, quantities, shipping routes, and delivery deadlines.

A central team may manage the campaign, while local suppliers manufacture or distribute products in different markets. Without consistent specifications and clear responsibilities, the result can be missed deadlines, inconsistent branding, incorrect quantities, and unnecessary freight costs.

This framework provides a structured process for managing merchandise from initial event planning through final delivery and inventory reconciliation.

## Objectives

- Plan merchandise requirements separately for each event location.
- Select appropriate products and production arrangements.
- Maintain consistent branding across suppliers and markets.
- Allocate quantities using attendance and distribution requirements.
- Coordinate production schedules and approval deadlines.
- Manage international shipping and customs requirements.
- Monitor deliveries and resolve delays.
- Reconcile unused merchandise after each event.

## 1. Event Requirements and Market Planning

Create a separate requirements record for every event location.

| Field | Required information |
|---|---|
| Event | Event name or campaign |
| Market | Country and city |
| Event date | Confirmed event date |
| Attendance | Expected or registered attendees |
| Merchandise | Products required |
| Quantity | Required units by product |
| Delivery deadline | Date merchandise must arrive |
| Delivery address | Complete receiving address |
| Local contact | Person responsible for receipt |
| Distribution | How merchandise will be handed out |
| Special requirements | Local handling or delivery restrictions |

Do not use one global quantity or delivery date when markets have different attendance levels and schedules.

## 2. Product Selection and Specification

Evaluate products against their intended use, audience, production requirements, and delivery conditions.

Assess:

- Product suitability and durability
- Material and construction
- Weight and packed dimensions
- Branding method and placement
- Size ranges and product variations
- Availability in each market
- Import restrictions and applicable duties
- Local production alternatives

Compare the full delivered cost, not just the purchase price.

Every approved product should have a documented specification. Include product description, material, dimensions, colour, branding method, artwork reference, and acceptable quality requirements.

## 3. Quantity Planning and Allocation

Calculate merchandise quantities separately for each market.

Use confirmed registrations where available. For less certain attendance, document the estimate and the assumptions behind it.

A basic allocation model is:

\[
Q_m = A_m \times R_m + B_m
\]

Where:

- \(Q_m\) = planned quantity for a market
- \(A_m\) = expected attendees
- \(R_m\) = expected distribution rate
- \(B_m\) = additional buffer quantity

**Illustrative example:**

| Market | Expected attendance | Distribution rate | Buffer | Planned quantity |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| United States | 500 | 90% | 25 | 475 |
| Germany | 300 | 90% | 15 | 285 |
| Singapore | 200 | 90% | 10 | 190 |
| **Total** | **1,000** | | **50** | **950** |

The distribution rate and buffer should reflect the event's registration data, product type, and distribution method. They are planning assumptions, not universal percentages.

Keep apparel sizes, colours, and other product variations in separate allocation records.

## 4. Production and Supplier Coordination

Choose production arrangements based on total cost, capacity, quality requirements, and delivery deadlines.

Two common models are:

| Model | Advantages | Considerations |
|---|---|---|
| Centralised production | Fewer production partners and more central control | International freight, customs, and longer transit |
| Regional production | Shorter delivery routes and local sourcing options | More supplier coordination and quality control |

A hybrid model may be appropriate when some products are available locally and others require centralised production.

Before confirming an order, document:

- Supplier and production location
- Approved product specifications
- Quantity by product and market
- Production capacity
- Unit and setup costs
- Production start and completion dates
- Quality inspection requirements
- Packing and dispatch arrangements

Confirm supplier commitments before releasing the order.

## 5. Artwork and Brand Control

Use one controlled source of approved brand files.

The central artwork record should contain:

- Approved logo files
- Colour references
- Typography requirements
- Logo dimensions and placement
- Product-specific artwork
- Approved file formats
- Revision numbers
- Approval dates
- Responsible approver

Every production partner must receive the correct approved version.

Request a production proof from each supplier or production location. Review colour, placement, dimensions, material, and decoration quality before authorising full production.

Do not rely on one supplier's proof to approve another supplier's process.

## 6. Production Scheduling

Work backwards from the required delivery date for each market.

Include every stage:

1. Product selection and specification
2. Supplier confirmation
3. Artwork preparation
4. Proof review and approval
5. Material procurement
6. Production
7. Quality inspection
8. Packing
9. Dispatch
10. Customs clearance, where applicable
11. Final delivery

Maintain separate planned and actual dates.

If a milestone slips, review its effect on the remaining schedule immediately. Identify whether the delivery deadline can still be met and whether an alternative production or shipping arrangement is required.

## 7. International Shipping and Delivery

Plan shipments by destination rather than treating the order as one global consignment.

Record:

- Shipment origin and destination
- Product descriptions and quantities
- Package dimensions and weight
- Transport method
- Dispatch date
- Tracking reference
- Commercial and shipping documentation
- Applicable import requirements
- Expected customs clearance
- Expected delivery date
- Local receiving contact

Confirm documentation and import requirements with the relevant logistics provider before dispatch.

Distinguish three milestones:

- **Production completion:** Goods are ready for dispatch.
- **Dispatch:** Goods leave the production or distribution facility.
- **Delivery:** Goods reach the agreed destination and are received.

A dispatch confirmation is not proof of delivery.

## 8. Delivery Monitoring and Contingency Management

Assign one person to monitor each shipment or destination.

Establish:
- Tracking review frequency
- Supplier and carrier contacts
- Escalation deadlines
- Delivery confirmation requirements
- Backup sourcing options
- Authority to approve additional costs

Use a defined escalation process when a shipment falls behind schedule.

Possible responses include local replacement production, alternative products, adjusted quantities, or revised distribution arrangements.

Record the decision, cost, responsible person, and effect on the event.

## 9. Post-Event Inventory

Reconcile merchandise after each event.

| Inventory field | Record |
|---|---|
| Initial quantity | Units delivered |
| Distributed quantity | Units handed out |
| Remaining quantity | Units not distributed |
| Damaged quantity | Units unsuitable for use |
| Storage location | Current stock location |
| Future allocation | Planned use at another event |
| Final disposition | Retained, donated, recycled, or disposed |

Calculate remaining stock:

\[
\text{Remaining Stock} =
\text{Delivered Quantity} - \text{Distributed Quantity} - \text{Damaged Quantity}
\]

Decide whether remaining products should be retained locally, transferred to another event, donated, or recycled. Include storage, handling, and transport costs in that decision.

## 10. Responsibility and Accountability

Each activity needs one accountable owner. Suppliers and logistics partners may perform the work, but internal responsibility should remain clear.

| Activity | Accountable owner | Supporting parties |
|---|---|---|
| Event requirements | Event manager | Local event teams |
| Product selection | Merchandise manager | Procurement, local teams |
| Quantity allocation | Event manager | Registration team |
| Supplier appointment | Procurement lead | Merchandise manager |
| Artwork approval | Brand manager | Design team, suppliers |
| Production release | Merchandise manager | Production supplier |
| Quality inspection | Quality lead | Supplier |
| Shipping arrangements | Logistics lead | Freight provider |
| Customs coordination | Logistics lead | Importer, customs broker |
| Delivery confirmation | Local event lead | Logistics provider |
| Inventory reconciliation | Merchandise manager | Local event teams |

Changes to product specifications, quantities, or delivery arrangements should be approved by the designated owner before implementation.

## 11. Risk Register

Review risks before production begins and update them throughout the project.

| Risk | Potential impact | Preventive control | Response |
|---|---|---|---|
| Late artwork approval | Production delay | Set approval deadlines | Escalate approval or revise schedule |
| Material unavailable | Delayed production | Confirm stock before order release | Approve an alternative material |
| Inconsistent branding | Different product appearance | Controlled artwork and local proofs | Correct before full production |
| Incorrect quantities | Shortage or surplus | Market-level allocation records | Arrange local replenishment |
| Customs delay | Late delivery | Verify documentation and import requirements | Coordinate with customs broker |
| Shipment delay | Merchandise misses event | Track shipments and set review dates | Use approved local alternatives |
| Damaged goods | Reduced usable stock | Appropriate packing and inspection | Arrange replacement or local sourcing |
| Excess inventory | Storage and disposal costs | Forecast by market | Reallocate, retain, donate, or recycle |

Prioritise risks by probability and impact. Assign an owner and review date to each significant risk.

## 12. Performance Measurement

Use consistent definitions across all event locations.

| KPI | Calculation | Purpose |
|---|---|---|
| On-time delivery rate | On-time deliveries ÷ total deliveries × 100 | Measures delivery reliability |
| Quantity accuracy | Correct units received ÷ units ordered × 100 | Measures allocation and fulfilment accuracy |
| Production lead time | Production completion date − production release date | Measures manufacturing duration |
| Approval lead time | Final approval date − proof submission date | Measures approval duration |
| Freight cost per unit | Total freight cost ÷ units shipped | Tracks transport cost |
| Merchandise utilisation | Units distributed ÷ units delivered × 100 | Measures distribution effectiveness |
| Quality acceptance rate | Accepted units ÷ inspected units × 100 | Measures product quality |
| Remaining inventory rate | Remaining usable units ÷ units delivered × 100 | Measures unused stock |

Calculate each KPI by market as well as across the full event. A global average can conceal a delivery or quality problem in one location.
